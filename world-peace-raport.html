FIELD NOTES / PEACE & CONFLICT

World Peace Report

A calm, human-readable look at where violence is growing, where diplomacy still has room to work, and what people can do next.

Report date: 18 November 2026 · Edition 001 · Independent editorial synthesis.

1. The short picture

The world is not moving in one direction. Some wars are seeing renewed diplomatic contact, but many civilians remain trapped between armed groups, blocked aid, damaged homes and fear. A ceasefire can pause the shooting; it does not by itself rebuild trust, homes, schools or a fair political future.

Three urgent patterns stand out: the fragile Gaza ceasefire, the continuing Russia–Ukraine war as winter approaches, and the enormous civilian cost of Sudan's war. Myanmar and eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo also deserve sustained attention rather than being forgotten when headlines move elsewhere.

2. Conflict watch

Gaza / Israel

A truce under strain

Reuters reports that the ceasefire framework is at risk: disarmament, Israeli withdrawal, civilian governance, reconstruction and aid remain unresolved. More than two million people remain largely displaced.

Peace opening: Turn the ceasefire into monitored reciprocal steps. Protect civilians and aid workers; agree on predictable aid access; establish a credible Palestinian civilian administration with local legitimacy; and connect security arrangements to a clear political horizon.

The safety and political rights of both Palestinians and Israelis must be treated as real, not as bargaining chips.

Ukraine / Russia

Talks without a breakthrough

Recent US, Ukrainian and Russian contacts have produced a prisoner-exchange track but no lasting ceasefire. European governments seek a place in future discussions while strikes on infrastructure continue and winter raises the danger to heating and electricity.

Peace opening: Start with verifiable steps: prisoner exchanges, return of unlawfully transferred civilians and children, and protection of nuclear and energy infrastructure. Any wider settlement must include Ukraine's voice and credible security guarantees.

Sudan

Civilians need protection now

The UN human-rights office says it confirmed more than 2,230 civilian deaths in Sudan from January through October 2026, with drone strikes responsible for many. Hospitals, markets, schools, water sources and displacement sites have been hit.

Peace opening: Press outside sponsors to stop arms and logistical support. Build a monitored humanitarian pause around aid routes and medical facilities, while a parallel Sudanese civilian-led dialogue addresses local power, safety, justice and the country's future.

Myanmar / Eastern DRC

Do not forget quieter headlines

Diplomatic reporting continues to flag Myanmar's violence and the need for humanitarian access and an inclusive political process. In eastern DR Congo, rival peace efforts have not yet brought lasting security to communities living with armed groups and displacement.

Peace opening: Protect local ceasefire monitors, aid corridors and community mediators. Include women, displaced people, local authorities and minority communities in talks, with clear ways to investigate violations.

3. What history teaches

The Good Friday Agreement in Northern Ireland (1998) did not make every disagreement disappear. It created institutions where former enemies could share power, recognised different identities, and gave political arguments a safer place than the battlefield. Its lesson is that peace needs rules, inclusion and patience.

The Dayton Agreement (1995) stopped the war in Bosnia and Herzegovina, showing the value of sustained diplomacy and a negotiated end to mass violence. But it also left a complicated political structure and deep divisions. Stopping a war is a beginning; a settlement must also help people live together and govern fairly.

Neither history offers a magic formula. Peace lasts longer when affected people have a voice, promises can be checked, and justice is not erased for the sake of a photograph.

4. A practical peace plan

  1. Stop immediate harm. Protect civilians, hospitals, schools, aid workers and essential services. Humanitarian access should never be used as a reward.
  2. Make small promises verifiable. Use neutral monitors, public timelines and clear ways to report violations. Begin with steps that save lives and can be checked.
  3. Bring missing voices to the table. Include women, local communities, displaced people, minorities and those who must live with the agreement.
  4. Separate justice from revenge. Investigate serious abuses fairly. Accountability should apply to every side; collective punishment prepares the next conflict.
  5. Offer a future worth choosing. Rebuild homes, schools, water systems and jobs. Give communities a meaningful say in local government and security.
  6. Make outside powers part of the solution. Stop feeding wars with weapons, money and propaganda. Use influence to support talks, not to turn local people into pieces on a chessboard.
A simple principle: A peace agreement should make ordinary life safer and more dignified for people on every side. If it only makes leaders comfortable, it is not finished.

5. A philosophical note

People often say peace begins when enemies stop fighting. That is only the first quiet moment. A deeper peace begins when people no longer have to fear that tomorrow will bring the same loss again.

We do not need to pretend that all sides are equally responsible, or that every demand is fair. We do need to remember that a child is not responsible for a government's choices, and grief does not become less real because it belongs to someone across a border.

Peace is not asking victims to forget. It is building a future in which fewer people become victims.
