FIELD NOTES / PEACE & CONFLICT

World Peace Report

A plain-language account of the latest conflict developments, the people affected, and practical ways to turn diplomacy into safer lives.

Report date: 20 November 2026 · Edition 003 · Source cut-off: 20 November 2026 · Independent editorial synthesis.

1. The short picture

There is one major sign of progress in international justice, but no broad end to the wars covered here. On 19 November, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled that Myanmar was responsible for genocide against the Rohingya during the 2017 military operations in northern Rakhine State. The judgment is final and binding. It is an important recognition for survivors, but it does not by itself stop today's fighting or make a safe return possible.

In Ukraine, a high-profile meeting of the US, Chinese and Russian leaders produced a general statement about avoiding nuclear war, but no clear Ukraine settlement or timetable for arms-control talks. European leaders again said that Ukraine and Europe must be part of decisions about Ukraine. In Gaza, a major Israeli tank operation ended, but families returned to damaged homes and the ceasefire remains fragile. Sudan's civilians continue to face deadly drone attacks. Regional governments are trying to prevent the Iran confrontation from widening. In eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), regional and UN leaders continue to demand a ceasefire, but the latest official joint statement we found does not establish that one is holding.

Editorial assessment: The central question is whether diplomatic promises and legal decisions will change what happens to ordinary people. A declaration, judgment or meeting matters most when it leads to protection, aid, accountability and a political future that affected communities can shape.

2. Conflict watch

Myanmar / Rohingya

A landmark genocide judgment — and a long road to safety

Verified legal development: On 19 November, the ICJ found that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya during the 2017 clearance operations in northern Rakhine State. The UN Secretary-General said the Court found killings, serious physical and mental harm, and measures intended to prevent births, with the intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya group. The UN's Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar says the Court ordered measures including ending violations, preventing a repeat, investigating and punishing those responsible, and providing reparations.

This ruling concerns the 2017 operations; it is not a ceasefire agreement for Myanmar's separate nationwide conflict, which escalated after the military coup in February 2021. More than a million Rohingya remain displaced outside Myanmar, and a court ruling cannot make return safe on its own. The UN human rights chief has stressed that return must be safe and sustainable, not forced.

Peace opening: Myanmar should comply fully with the judgment, preserve evidence, investigate crimes and provide real remedies to survivors. Other states can support accountability and humanitarian aid, avoid sending equipment that enables abuses, and reject forced returns. Any return must be voluntary, safe and dignified, with rights, citizenship, family reunification and independent monitoring addressed.

Ukraine / Russia

Nuclear words, but no Ukraine peace deal

Verified reporting: Reuters reported that the 18 November meeting of US President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin produced a declaration saying nuclear war must never be fought. It did not set a timetable for arms-control negotiations or provide a concrete settlement for Ukraine. On 20 November, the Associated Press reported that US envoys briefed European leaders in Berlin on possible security guarantees; statements about what Russia might accept remain the envoys' assessment, not a confirmed Russian commitment.

The war began with Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine verified at least 372 civilians killed and 1,981 injured in October 2026, while warning that the actual toll may be higher.

Peace opening: Ukraine must be at the centre of decisions about its territory and future. Include European partners in security discussions, agree on monitored steps to protect civilians and energy systems, exchange prisoners, return unlawfully transferred civilians and children, and build guarantees that can be checked rather than relying on private assurances.

Gaza / Israel

A tank raid ends; the ceasefire still needs work

Verified reporting: Reuters reported on 19 November that Israel said its operation west of the ceasefire line had ended. Gaza residents began returning to Nuseirat, where some found damaged roads and homes. Gaza's government media office said about 6,000 people had fled during the operation; that figure is attributed to the office. Gaza health officials separately reported two Palestinians killed by tank shelling in Gaza City on Thursday; Reuters said Israel had not immediately commented.

A report presented to the UN Security Council by the Board of Peace warned that the ceasefire could collapse unless Israel and Hamas carry out their commitments. It pointed to continuing strikes, military activity, restrictions on supplies and delays over interim civilian arrangements. The UN humanitarian office's 13 November report said flooding had affected about 46,000 households and damaged more than 40,000 family shelters.

Peace opening: Put the ceasefire line and incident hotline under credible monitoring; ensure predictable entry of food, medicine, shelter materials and fuel; protect civilians and aid workers; and move hostage, detainee and remains issues through lawful, verifiable arrangements. A durable future needs legitimate Palestinian civilian governance and a credible political path, while addressing Israeli security concerns without collective punishment or permanent displacement.

Sudan

Drones put homes and essential services at risk

Verified UN reporting: The UN Human Rights Office reported on 17 November that at least 1,390 civilians were killed in Sudan's war between January and June 2026, with more than 80 per cent of those deaths caused by drone strikes. Its report describes attacks on markets, hospitals, schools, fuel supplies, water sources, displacement sites and civilian vehicles. It also recorded 29 drone strikes attributed to the Rapid Support Forces on electricity facilities and fuel stations across 11 states between 20 October and 4 November; the resulting blackouts and fuel shortages disrupted health care, clean water and food production.

The war between Sudan's armed forces and the Rapid Support Forces began in April 2023. Both sides have been accused of serious violations. Responsibility for each incident must be judged from evidence, not assumed from the identity of the accused.

Peace opening: Stop attacks on civilians and infrastructure, allow impartial investigations, protect aid routes and local relief workers, and press outside suppliers to stop fuelling the war. Local humanitarian pauses can save lives while Sudanese civilian groups, including women and displaced communities, shape a national process for accountable government and security.

Iran / Gulf

Regional diplomacy tries to prevent a wider war

Verified reporting: The Associated Press reported on 19 November that Gulf states, Pakistan and Turkey were increasing diplomatic efforts with Washington to prevent renewed all-out conflict involving Iran. The report described recent attacks and fears that oil and power facilities, water-desalination plants, undersea cables and shipping routes could be affected. These are reported diplomatic efforts and risk assessments, not proof that a wider war is inevitable.

On 13 November, a UN political affairs official warned the Security Council that incidents involving commercial vessels and restrictions on navigation in the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab were creating serious risks for regional stability and the global economy.

Peace opening: Reopen military hotlines, investigate maritime incidents independently, protect commercial crews and civilian infrastructure, and agree on rules against attacks on ships and energy or water systems. Any wider talks should include regional states and verifiable steps on security, sanctions and nuclear risks.

Eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo

Regional demands are clear; implementation is not

Verified official position: In a joint statement published by the African Union on 2 November after its 23 October meeting with the UN Security Council, the two bodies called for an immediate end to hostilities, protection of civilians and aid workers, and safe humanitarian access. They called for implementation of existing commitments, including withdrawal of Rwandan forces from DRC territory, an end to support for M23, an end to Congolese military support for armed groups such as the FDLR, and dismantling M23's parallel administration. These are the bodies' stated demands; they should not be mistaken for independent proof of every allegation.

The roots of the conflict include the aftermath of the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, the regional Congo wars, unresolved security and citizenship questions, and competition over land and mineral wealth. The Doha and Washington tracks address different parts of the crisis and need to work alongside African Union mediation.

Peace opening: Make the ceasefire measurable, monitored and public; coordinate the different diplomatic tracks; stop cross-border support for armed groups; protect civilians and aid access; and include local communities, women, displaced people and civil society in decisions about return, justice, security and resource governance. We did not find a verified announcement of a new, functioning ceasefire by this report's cut-off.

3. What history can teach

The Rohingya judgment shows that documenting crimes and bringing evidence before an independent court can matter, even when years have passed. But legal accountability and physical protection are different jobs: the judgment must be implemented, and survivors need safety, rights and support now.

The Good Friday Agreement in Northern Ireland (1998) and the Dayton Agreement in Bosnia and Herzegovina (1995) show how sustained diplomacy can stop large-scale fighting. They also show that a signed agreement is not the end of the work. Institutions, public participation, security guarantees, justice and everyday services determine whether people can build a shared future. No historic agreement can simply be copied into another country.

One lesson links these examples: a peace process is more durable when people who live with its consequences can influence it, when promises can be independently checked, and when victims are not asked to trade away their rights for a photograph or a temporary pause in fighting.

4. Practical steps that could reduce violence

  1. Implement the Myanmar judgment. Preserve evidence, investigate crimes, provide reparations and protect survivors from intimidation. Do not force Rohingya refugees to return before safety, rights and dignity can be guaranteed.
  2. Do not negotiate Ukraine away. Keep Ukraine involved in every decision about Ukraine; include European security partners; and begin with steps that can be independently verified, such as prisoner exchanges and protection of civilians and energy infrastructure.
  3. Make Gaza's ceasefire visible in daily life. Monitor the line and violations, open predictable aid routes, repair essential services, protect civilians on all sides and create a credible, inclusive Palestinian civilian administration.
  4. Protect Sudan's civilian lifelines. Stop drone attacks on markets, hospitals, power, fuel and water; secure humanitarian pauses; and make outside support for armed parties a subject of public scrutiny and restraint.
  5. Build a Gulf de-escalation channel. Use direct hotlines and neutral investigations to prevent maritime incidents from triggering wider attacks. Keep civilian shipping, water and energy infrastructure out of military bargaining.
  6. Coordinate the eastern DRC peace tracks. Link regional commitments to independent monitoring, cross-border restraint, humanitarian access and local participation, with public reporting on what each party actually implements.
  7. Pair accountability with repair. Investigate serious abuses impartially, protect witnesses, reunite families and rebuild homes, schools, clinics, water systems and livelihoods. Justice should prevent future harm, not become collective revenge.
A humane test: Does a proposal make an ordinary family's next week safer, and can people verify that it happened? If not, it is not yet enough.

5. A philosophical note

Justice and peace are not enemies. A lasting peace cannot require survivors to deny what happened, and justice cannot be reduced to punishment alone. It should also restore dignity, protect the living and make another atrocity less likely.

People are not their governments, armies or armed groups. A child should not inherit guilt for a leader's decision. The work of peace is to make room for truth, safety, rights and a future in which disagreement no longer has to be settled by force.

Edition 002 — 19 November 2026
FIELD NOTES / PEACE & CONFLICT

World Peace Report

A plain-language snapshot of the wars, risks and diplomatic openings shaping the world — with people, not power, at the centre.

Report date: 19 November 2026 · Edition 002 · Source cut-off: 19 November 2026 · Independent editorial synthesis.

1. The short picture

Diplomacy has not disappeared, but it is struggling to change what armed forces do on the ground. Gaza's ceasefire is being tested by renewed operations and a severe humanitarian situation. Ukraine faces rising civilian harm while several diplomatic tracks compete for attention. Sudan's war is taking an especially heavy toll through drone attacks. A lull in the Iran confrontation has broken into new exchanges, putting regional security and shipping at risk. In Myanmar and eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), regional and international peace efforts have yet to produce safety for many civilians.

Editorial assessment: The common warning is a widening gap between agreements made by leaders and the daily reality of people living near the fighting. This is analysis, not a formal ranking of conflicts. The casualty figures below come from different reporting systems and should not be compared as if they were measured in the same way.

2. Conflict watch

Gaza / Israel

A ceasefire under renewed pressure

Verified reporting: Reuters reported on 18 November that Israeli forces began a ground operation in central Gaza, crossing the ceasefire's Yellow Line into an area from which they had withdrawn under the October 2025 truce. Israel said the operation targeted a tunnel it considered a threat; Hamas called it a ceasefire violation. Medics reported deaths in incidents around the operation, while Israeli officials disputed or qualified parts of the account. The claims and casualty details are attributed to the parties and sources Reuters quoted.

In its 13 November update, the UN humanitarian office (OCHA) said flooding had affected about 46,000 households and damaged more than 40,000 family shelters. For 3–9 November, it reported that Gaza's Ministry of Health had recorded 22 Palestinians killed and 90 injured in conflict-related incidents. OCHA also described shortages and disruption affecting health, education, shelter and water services.

Peace opening: Restore a monitored ceasefire line and a direct incident hotline; make aid entry predictable and safe; protect civilians, medical teams and aid workers; and move hostage, detainee and remains issues through lawful, verifiable arrangements. A durable settlement also needs legitimate Palestinian civilian governance and a credible political future, alongside enforceable arrangements addressing Israeli security concerns.

Ukraine / Russia

More civilian harm, more diplomatic tracks

Verified reporting: On 17 November, the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine said it had verified at least 372 civilians killed and 1,981 injured in October 2026. That was 95 per cent more casualties than in October 2025. The mission said the first ten months of 2026 had already seen more civilian deaths than all of 2025.

On 19 November, Ukraine's European partners were meeting in Berlin with US envoys to discuss security guarantees and the next stage of negotiations. A separate meeting involving the US, Russian and Chinese leaders was expected in China the same day. A leaders' meeting can create an opening, but Ukraine's future cannot be settled legitimately without Ukraine's participation.

Peace opening: Put Ukraine at the centre of every decision about Ukraine. Begin with verifiable steps such as prisoner exchanges, the return of unlawfully transferred civilians and children, protection of nuclear and energy infrastructure, and a monitored halt to attacks on civilians. Any settlement needs credible security guarantees, a clear process for disputed issues and reconstruction that allows people to return safely.

Sudan

Drones are multiplying the civilian danger

Verified reporting: The UN Human Rights Office said on 17 November that it had confirmed at least 2,230 civilian deaths in Sudan between January and the end of October 2026; more than 1,630 were attributed to drone strikes. The UN warned that the real toll is likely higher. It described attacks affecting markets, health facilities, schools, fuel supplies, water sources, civilian vehicles and displacement sites. Kordofan accounted for more than two-thirds of the documented civilian casualties.

The war between Sudan's armed forces and the Rapid Support Forces began in April 2023. Years of political exclusion, armed competition and weak civilian institutions form part of the background, but they do not excuse attacks on civilians by any party.

Peace opening: Outside governments should stop supplying weapons and logistical support that prolong the war. A monitored humanitarian pause should protect aid routes, hospitals, markets and water systems. Local ceasefires and community mediators can reduce immediate harm, while a civilian-led national process addresses security, justice, political power and a path back to accountable government.

Iran / Gulf

A fragile lull has broken

Verified reporting: The Associated Press reported on 18 November that Iran had launched its third attack in five days on US military targets in Bahrain, with drones aimed at a radar installation. Bahrain and the US said their defences intercepted the attack without damage or injuries. The AP also reported a US strike on an Iranian missile site on Qeshm Island; local officials said there was no damage to civilian areas or infrastructure. These details are attributed to the officials and reporting cited by AP.

The exchanges ended roughly two months of relative calm and raised fears that the US-Israel-Iran confrontation could widen again. Commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz remains exposed to the conflict; Reuters reported on 13 November that Iran's Revolutionary Guards said an LNG tanker had struck a mine, but immediate details about the vessel were not available.

Peace opening: Re-establish direct military and diplomatic hotlines, agree that commercial ships and civilian infrastructure are not targets, and support neutral investigation of maritime incidents. A phased agreement could address the Strait of Hormuz, sanctions, regional attacks and Iran's nuclear programme, with meaningful verification by the International Atomic Energy Agency and regional states involved in the process.

Myanmar

Regional leaders still see no real breakthrough

Verified reporting: In its 15 November review of the Five-Point Consensus, ASEAN leaders expressed deep concern about the conflict and humanitarian situation in Myanmar. They denounced violence against civilians and public facilities, urged all parties to halt indiscriminate violence, called for civilian protection and humanitarian access, and reaffirmed the need for inclusive national political dialogue.

The present crisis escalated after the military takeover in February 2021. It involves the military, long-standing ethnic armed organisations and newer resistance groups; civilians face air attacks, displacement, detention and restricted access to help. A political process that excludes major communities is unlikely to produce a lasting settlement.

Peace opening: Turn ASEAN's commitments into a public timetable: measurable reductions in attacks, safe and sustained aid access, protection for local relief workers, and dialogue that includes ethnic communities, women, young people, political prisoners' representatives and the Rohingya. Humanitarian aid must reach people based on need, not political loyalty.

Eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo

Two peace tracks, too little safety

Verified reporting: Al Jazeera reported on 18 November that fighting continued in North and South Kivu despite the Washington and Doha diplomatic processes. It described further displacement, disruption to essential services and local frustration that repeated meetings have not brought security. The two tracks address different parts of the problem: regional tensions between the DRC and Rwanda, and the confrontation between the Congolese government and armed groups.

In an October 2026 joint statement, the African Union Peace and Security Council and UN Security Council called for an immediate end to hostilities, protection of civilians and humanitarian workers, safe aid access, and implementation of existing commitments. They called for the withdrawal of Rwandan forces and an end to support for M23, while also calling for Congolese forces to stop supporting other armed groups, particularly the FDLR. Those are demands in the joint statement, not a substitute for independently checking each allegation.

The roots reach back decades, including the 1994 genocide in neighbouring Rwanda, the regional Congo wars of 1996 and 1998, and unresolved disputes over land, citizenship, security and control of resources. Peace opening: Coordinate the Washington and Doha processes, make ceasefire monitoring independent and public, stop cross-border support for armed groups, and include Congolese civil society, women, young people and local communities in decisions about security, return, justice and resource governance.

3. What history can teach

The conflicts in this report did not begin with this week's headlines. Myanmar's current national crisis accelerated after the 2021 coup. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. Sudan's current war began in April 2023. Eastern Congo's violence is tied to older regional wars and unresolved local grievances. The Iran confrontation has also shown how a regional military crisis can threaten trade routes and people far from the immediate front.

History does not offer a copy-and-paste peace deal. It does offer a warning: agreements made only by armed leaders can leave local fears, political exclusion and impunity untouched. Monitoring, fair rules, public participation and a realistic political future are not decorative extras; they are what help a ceasefire become a peace.

4. Practical steps that could reduce violence

  1. Make ceasefires observable. Publish the agreed lines, times and rules; use neutral monitors and shared hotlines; and investigate alleged breaches promptly. A promise that cannot be checked is easy to break.
  2. Protect the things people need to live. Hospitals, schools, water systems, electricity, food markets, shelters, aid workers and commercial ships must not become bargaining chips or targets.
  3. Start with life-saving steps. Exchange prisoners, return unlawfully transferred civilians and children, recover and return remains, open humanitarian routes and restore essential services. These steps can be verified and can build a little trust.
  4. Include people who are usually left out. Women, displaced families, young people, minorities, local authorities and civil society should help shape the agreement, not just be asked to accept it afterward.
  5. Reduce outside fuel for wars. States should stop supplying weapons, money, intelligence or logistics to groups committing abuses. Apply pressure to decision-makers without collectively punishing ordinary people.
  6. Pair justice with repair. Preserve evidence, investigate serious violations impartially, protect witnesses and provide remedies for victims. Rebuild homes, clinics, schools, water and livelihoods so returning home is a real option.
  7. Give peace a political destination. Address security fears, rights, representation, sovereignty and local power openly. Do not confuse silence imposed by force with reconciliation.
A humane test: Does this step make an ordinary family's next week safer, and can people check that it happened? If not, it is not yet enough.

5. A philosophical note

Peace is not the same as pretending that every side has done the same things. Responsibility must be judged from evidence, and people who suffer should not be asked to forget or excuse abuse. But a child is not a government, a family is not an army, and a whole population should never be made to pay for the decisions of leaders or armed groups.

The first duty of peace is to stop preventable harm. The deeper work is to build a life in which people can disagree, remember, seek justice and still feel safe enough to share a future.

Edition 001 — 18 November 2026 (original edition)
FIELD NOTES / PEACE & CONFLICT

World Peace Report

A calm, human-readable look at where violence is growing, where diplomacy still has room to work, and what people can do next.

Report date: 18 November 2026 · Edition 001 · Independent editorial synthesis.

1. The short picture

The world is not moving in one direction. Some wars are seeing renewed diplomatic contact, but many civilians remain trapped between armed groups, blocked aid, damaged homes and fear. A ceasefire can pause the shooting; it does not by itself rebuild trust, homes, schools or a fair political future.

Three urgent patterns stand out: the fragile Gaza ceasefire, the continuing Russia–Ukraine war as winter approaches, and the enormous civilian cost of Sudan's war. Myanmar and eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo also deserve sustained attention rather than being forgotten when headlines move elsewhere.

2. Conflict watch

Gaza / Israel

A truce under strain

Reuters reports that the ceasefire framework is at risk: disarmament, Israeli withdrawal, civilian governance, reconstruction and aid remain unresolved. More than two million people remain largely displaced.

Peace opening: Turn the ceasefire into monitored reciprocal steps. Protect civilians and aid workers; agree on predictable aid access; establish a credible Palestinian civilian administration with local legitimacy; and connect security arrangements to a clear political horizon.

The safety and political rights of both Palestinians and Israelis must be treated as real, not as bargaining chips.

Ukraine / Russia

Talks without a breakthrough

Recent US, Ukrainian and Russian contacts have produced a prisoner-exchange track but no lasting ceasefire. European governments seek a place in future discussions while strikes on infrastructure continue and winter raises the danger to heating and electricity.

Peace opening: Start with verifiable steps: prisoner exchanges, return of unlawfully transferred civilians and children, and protection of nuclear and energy infrastructure. Any wider settlement must include Ukraine's voice and credible security guarantees.

Sudan

Civilians need protection now

The UN human-rights office says it confirmed more than 2,230 civilian deaths in Sudan from January through October 2026, with drone strikes responsible for many. Hospitals, markets, schools, water sources and displacement sites have been hit.

Peace opening: Press outside sponsors to stop arms and logistical support. Build a monitored humanitarian pause around aid routes and medical facilities, while a parallel Sudanese civilian-led dialogue addresses local power, safety, justice and the country's future.

Myanmar / Eastern DRC

Do not forget quieter headlines

Diplomatic reporting continues to flag Myanmar's violence and the need for humanitarian access and an inclusive political process. In eastern DR Congo, rival peace efforts have not yet brought lasting security to communities living with armed groups and displacement.

Peace opening: Protect local ceasefire monitors, aid corridors and community mediators. Include women, displaced people, local authorities and minority communities in talks, with clear ways to investigate violations.

3. What history teaches

The Good Friday Agreement in Northern Ireland (1998) did not make every disagreement disappear. It created institutions where former enemies could share power, recognised different identities, and gave political arguments a safer place than the battlefield. Its lesson is that peace needs rules, inclusion and patience.

The Dayton Agreement (1995) stopped the war in Bosnia and Herzegovina, showing the value of sustained diplomacy and a negotiated end to mass violence. But it also left a complicated political structure and deep divisions. Stopping a war is a beginning; a settlement must also help people live together and govern fairly.

Neither history offers a magic formula. Peace lasts longer when affected people have a voice, promises can be checked, and justice is not erased for the sake of a photograph.

4. A practical peace plan

  1. Stop immediate harm. Protect civilians, hospitals, schools, aid workers and essential services. Humanitarian access should never be used as a reward.
  2. Make small promises verifiable. Use neutral monitors, public timelines and clear ways to report violations. Begin with steps that save lives and can be checked.
  3. Bring missing voices to the table. Include women, local communities, displaced people, minorities and those who must live with the agreement.
  4. Separate justice from revenge. Investigate serious abuses fairly. Accountability should apply to every side; collective punishment prepares the next conflict.
  5. Offer a future worth choosing. Rebuild homes, schools, water systems and jobs. Give communities a meaningful say in local government and security.
  6. Make outside powers part of the solution. Stop feeding wars with weapons, money and propaganda. Use influence to support talks, not to turn local people into pieces on a chessboard.
A simple principle: A peace agreement should make ordinary life safer and more dignified for people on every side. If it only makes leaders comfortable, it is not finished.

5. A philosophical note

People often say peace begins when enemies stop fighting. That is only the first quiet moment. A deeper peace begins when people no longer have to fear that tomorrow will bring the same loss again.

We do not need to pretend that all sides are equally responsible, or that every demand is fair. We do need to remember that a child is not responsible for a government's choices, and grief does not become less real because it belongs to someone across a border.

Peace is not asking victims to forget. It is building a future in which fewer people become victims.
